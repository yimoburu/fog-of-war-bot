# Strength research and arena calibration

Research date: November 12, 2026, America/Los_Angeles. This note records public-source research and local evidence inspected during development. It does not certify a rating, reproduce an external engine's published results, or report an arena submission.

The 2200–2500 target should be interpreted as **MIT arena Elo**, unless a different rating pool is explicitly chosen. Ratings from ordinary chess, Chess.com Fog of War, research matches, and the MIT bot ladder are not interchangeable. The current candidate has no measured MIT arena rating.

The official client states that the baseline remains at 1000, other teams start at 1500, and every rated game updates permanent team Elo with K=8. Fewer than 20 games is provisional. Elo carries across versions: `activeRecord` isolates the active version's W/D/L, while rating and lifetime counts do not. Historical reconstruction uses today's K=8 and can differ from ratings calculated under earlier rules. A final strength claim must name the team, date, active version, game sample and carryover caveat. [API documentation](https://www.mit.edu/~6.7980/fow/api.html) · [Official client source inspected](https://www.mit.edu/~6.7980/fow/assets/main-By_mfwSY.js)

The completed local development artifact records **12 wins in 12 games**: six against `legacy_bot.py` and six against `baseline.py`, with three games in each color per opponent, no reported failures, seed 21980, and 180+2 time control. These are development results tied to the source hashes in that artifact; subsequent edits require fresh validation. The run used macOS ARM64 and Python 3.13.2, not the Linux arena. Its `absolute_arena_elo` is explicitly null. [Development evaluation artifact](../outputs/development-search-21980.json)

This small, saturated sample cannot establish the requested rating. Six wins against one opponent do not produce a finite maximum-likelihood Elo difference, and a relative logistic score transform is not an official ladder rating. The baseline's fixed 1000 label does not make a small local match a reliable bridge to 2200–2500, particularly across different runtimes and opponent distributions. Holdout results should be reported separately from games used to guide development; this note does not anticipate their outcome.

The current official runtime advertises Linux amd64, Python 3.14.8/C++/Rust, **two physical cores/four threads shared by both bots**, **4 GiB per bot**, a five-minute offline build, and a 180-second clock with two-second increments. The opponent is paused during one's turn. Both build and play lack network access. Older search-engine snapshots still show 1 CPU/2 GiB and an unopened preview; those are stale relative to the inspected application. [Official challenge and runtime](https://www.mit.edu/~6.7980/fow/) · [Current application asset](https://www.mit.edu/~6.7980/fow/assets/main-By_mfwSY.js)

The provided referee is the interoperability reference. It ends a game immediately when the player to move can capture the previous mover's king; an additional capture is not required. It then applies repetition, the 100-halfmove draw, and no-move loss rules. Castling ignores attacked squares. Observation processing can occur after a frozen bot resumes, so its cost belongs inside the same turn budget as inference and search. Local trusted execution does not reproduce that isolation. [Official starter package](https://www.mit.edu/~6.7980/fow/starter/starter.zip) · [Local unmodified referee](../referee.py)

The strongest relevant research found is **Obscuro**, described by Brian Hu Zhang and Tuomas Sandholm in their ICLR 2026 paper. It maintains possible full positions, samples search roots, solves knowledge-limited subgames with predictive CFR+, expands a growing tree, reuses prior search, and evaluates leaves with Stockfish. Its reported strong results support this architectural direction, not an MIT Elo conversion. Human matches used a six-core desktop; AI experiments used five cores per side. The five-second search allowance excludes position enumeration, a material difference from MIT's total game clock. No original released implementation was located in this research. [Primary paper, version 2](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.01242v2)

**Misty** is the clearest public implementation candidate found: a GPL-3.0-or-later Python/Rust engine inspired by Obscuro, with Stockfish integration and a documented JSONL protocol. Its upstream protocol is not MIT's raw-line protocol. Adapting it requires observation, move-history, castling, en-passant, terminal-rule and timing parity checks, an offline Linux build, and review of redistribution requirements. The generic rules interface inspected in the repository explicitly was not yet wired into all engine components. No MIT rating was verified. [Misty repository](https://github.com/brianhliou/misty) · [Protocol](https://github.com/brianhliou/misty/blob/main/docs/protocol/engine-protocol.md) · [Rules implementation](https://github.com/brianhliou/misty/blob/main/src/fow_chess/rules.py)

Misty's author reports a production cap of 16 million belief positions, a 7.7-GB peak, and decisions taking 9–16 seconds against a nominal five-second budget. Sampling can discard the true position, after which propagation alone cannot recover it. Those are reasons to benchmark and constrain an adaptation rather than assume its default profile fits MIT. The author does not offer a validated numerical Elo. These are author-reported findings, not measurements performed here. [Author's implementation and failure analysis](https://brianhliou.com/posts/misty-fog-of-war-engine/)

Another public implementation, **opowell/obscuro-chess**, supplies a JavaScript engine with belief reasoning and a learned opponent move prior. It is useful as a comparator or source of ideas; its runtime and protocol require adaptation, and no MIT rating or direct compatibility was verified. A bounded search did not locate downloadable submissions for the leading MIT teams. Public completed replays are available, but the documented public API does not supply those teams' source archives. [obscuro-chess repository](https://github.com/opowell/obscuro-chess) · [MIT public API](https://www.mit.edu/~6.7980/fow/api.html)

**LiteEFG DarkChess** explicitly implements MIT's game in pure JAX and provides a PPO example. It is useful for accelerated self-play and future training. Actor inputs use the player's observations; a privileged full-state critic is training-only. Its finite observation window is an abstraction rather than perfect recall. Neither the environment nor the example establishes a pretrained strong policy or an arena rating. [Author's environment documentation](https://liumy2010.github.io/LiteEFG/guide/deep-learning/dark-chess.html) · [PPO example](https://github.com/liumy2010/LiteEFG/blob/main/examples/dark_chess_ppo.py)

The following are engineering recommendations inferred from the evidence:

1. Preserve protocol and referee parity first, including every-ply observations, negative information from the visibility mask, castling, en passant, immediate king-loss semantics and draws. Give observation updates, recovery and search a shared clock budget and retain a valid fallback.
2. Measure belief quality, not only wins. Track depletion, approximate recovery, sampled support, and offline true-position coverage. Current-view recovery must remain distinguishable from positions consistent with the full observed history. Replay true boards are valid offline labels, never live policy inputs.
3. Compare the current candidate with the frozen legacy bot, several opponent styles, and an adapted stronger open engine. Hold out seeds and games before tuning. Record source hashes, colors, failures, peak memory and tail latency; avoid interpreting a clean run on one host as arena qualification.
4. For substantial further strength, investigate efficient information-set search, improved evaluation, opponent priors and tree reuse. Shared root decisions over determinized worlds still permit future strategy fusion; this is an approximation, not CFR or an equilibrium guarantee. Native hot paths may be necessary for broader beliefs/search within two cores and 4 GiB.
5. Use completed MIT replays to evaluate stronger opponents' behavior and fit priors, with train/test separation by whole game, version and time. Respect documented API throttling and distinguish played versions from currently active versions.

Actual rating measurement requires a team authorized by course staff and a version admitted to the live ladder. The user can sign in privately through the instructor-provided team link; no token needs to be placed in this repository or research note. The inspected official client validates the team session with authenticated `GET /api/submissions`, sends the ZIP as multipart field `file` to `POST /api/submissions?autoActivate=...`, and can activate a validated version using `POST /api/submissions/{id}/activate`. Authentication uses a bearer team token. These calls were inspected, not executed. Prefer the official Submit interface because the public API reference covers read-only endpoints and the private client contract can change. [Official Submit interface](https://www.mit.edu/~6.7980/fow/?tab=submit) · [Inspected client implementation](https://www.mit.edu/~6.7980/fow/assets/main-By_mfwSY.js)

The archive needs root `run.sh`, optional `build.sh`, and unavailable dependencies/weights bundled for offline use. The arena builds and runs baseline tests in both colors. The client offers automatic activation after validation; leaving it enabled joins the ladder without a later activation step. Inspect build/test warnings as well as the pass/fail label: the current guide says timeouts and confirmed out-of-memory kills in test games can be warnings. Validation is therefore necessary but not proof of reliable play or target strength. [Official submission guide](https://www.mit.edu/~6.7980/fow/)

After activation, collect rated results over both colors and several comparable-strength opponents. Preserve team ID, submitted-version ID, archive/source hashes, played versions, time control, failures, and observation date. Assigned games can finish using a previous version after activation, and all still affect permanent team Elo. Report the live team rating alongside the active version's record and an uncertainty-aware analysis of that version's games. Twenty games only clears the site's provisional label; it does not by itself substantiate a narrow 2200–2500 claim. [Official API record definitions](https://www.mit.edu/~6.7980/fow/api.html)
